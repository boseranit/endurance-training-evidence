# Intensity distribution, periodization, and recovery

**Last checked:** 2026-10-02

This brief covers where those sessions sit in the week and the season.

## Durable conclusions

<a id="tid-c01"></a>
### TID-C01 — “80/20” describes a common high-volume pattern; it is not an invariant optimum

**Classification:** Moderate evidence that many successful endurance athletes who train most days put roughly three-quarters or more of *sessions* at low intensity, with a smaller share of sessions dominated by harder work ([Seiler and Kjerland, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0838.2004.00418.x); [Seiler, 2010](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.5.3.276)). **Mostly coaching practice** that 80/20, as a percentage of time or sessions, is the optimal prescription at 7–12 h/week or at every phase and event.

**Support.** In 11 nationally competitive junior male cross-country skiers over 32 days, heart-rate time was about 75/8/17% across three zones anchored to ventilatory thresholds, session RPE about 76/6/18%, and a lactate subset about 71/7/22% ([Seiler and Kjerland, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0838.2004.00418.x)). Seiler’s 2010 synthesis described athletes training about 10–13 times per week as self-organizing toward ~80% of *sessions* below about 2 mmol/L lactate and ~20% of sessions dominated by high-intensity work. That is observational description plus expert synthesis, not a dose–response trial of 70/30 vs 80/20 vs 90/10.

**Contradiction / limit.** WorldTour riders in the 10 weeks before a season, quantified by time in zone, were predominantly **pyramidal**, not polarized ([Mateo-March et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.5114/biolsport.2026.156234)). Elite running reviews likewise find both pyramidal and polarized organization, varying by event and phase ([Casado et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2021-0435); [Haugen et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01692-1)). No retrieved trial identifies 80 as a unique optimum.

**Practical implication.** Use “most training is easy, and hard work is deliberate and limited” as the principle. Do not audit an amateur week against 80% of a professional’s hours.

**Uncertainty.** The descriptive studies are mostly male, mostly not road cyclists, and use different zone anchors. A cyclist can match 80/20 by session count and miss it by time in zone in the same week.

<a id="tid-c02"></a>
### TID-C02 — Polarized training has not been shown to be the best distribution for trained cyclists’ time-trial performance

**Classification:** Moderate evidence that, in the syntheses available through early 2025, a polarized *label* is not superior to pyramidal or other non-polarized programs for time-trial performance. A small VO₂peak advantage in broader endurance meta-analyses is **Plausible but uncertain** as a reason to prefer polarized cycling plans. The older claim that polarized is clearly better than threshold-heavy training is **Moderate evidence** only for that narrower comparison, and it rests on few, small trials.

**Support for a limited polarized edge.** [Rosenblat, Perrotta, and Vicenzino, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000002618) pooled randomized trials of polarized vs threshold models in endurance-trained athletes (VO₂max/peak >50 mL·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹, >2 years’ training). Four studies met qualitative criteria; three entered the time-trial meta-analysis. PEDro scores were 4–5/10. Time-trial performance favored polarized (ES −0.66, 95% CI −1.17 to −0.15). Search date was 6 November 2016. [Neal et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00652.2012), one of the few trained-cyclist comparisons, is summarized below. [Silva Oliveira, Boppre, and Fonseca, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02034-z) (search to 20 October 2022; 17 studies, n=437) found polarized superior for VO₂peak (SMD 0.24, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.48; 11 studies, n=284) but not for time trial (SMD −0.01, 95% CI −0.28 to 0.25; n=221), time to exhaustion, or speed/power at VT2/LT2. The VO₂peak edge was confined, in their subgrouping, to interventions shorter than 12 weeks (SMD 0.40, 95% CI 0.08 to 0.71; n=163) and highly trained athletes (SMD 0.46, 95% CI 0.10 to 0.82; n=125).

**Newer comparisons do not crown a winner.** [Rosenblat et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02149-3) individual-participant network meta-analysis (13 studies, 348 athletes, 296 men and 52 women; search to 11 February 2024) found no difference between polarized and pyramidal training on time-in-heart-rate-zone analysis for VO₂max (SMD −0.06, p=0.68) or time trial (SMD −0.05, p=0.34), and no significant difference between polarized and the other distributions examined. A subgroup interaction suggested competitive athletes might gain more VO₂max from polarized training and recreational athletes from pyramidal training (SMD −0.63, p<0.05). That interaction was reported for VO₂max, not as a time-trial result. [Cove et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsams.2024.12.005) is the cyclist-specific multilevel meta-analysis. The abstract reports 41 studies, 81 groups, and 797 participants, with interventions longer than 4 weeks. The search closed on 23 October 2023; 2025 is the issue year. Methods section 2.2 used sex-specific Tier 3 *study-mean* VO₂max thresholds (men ≥59 mL·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹, women ≥54.4), not a rule that every participant was ≥59; the abstract simplifies that. Usable summary statistics are the abstract point estimates only: training improved VO₂max (g=0.42) and time-trial performance (g=0.39), with no significant between-model difference. Study counts and confidence intervals disagree inside the paper (abstract 41 studies; discussion 44; 23 randomized plus 21 non-randomized), so those intervals and alternate counts are not used. Certainty was graded low.

**Important classification limit, verified in the Cove full text.** Cove did **not** require an 80/20 time-in-zone distribution. “Polarized” meant low-intensity or habitual training *plus intervals shorter than 5 minutes* at the highest sustainable intensity or above maximal lactate steady state. Intervals longer than 5 minutes were classed non-polarized. That rule puts several well-known threshold-interval and 4×8 / 4×16 interventions on the non-polarized side even when the weekly pattern was otherwise low-intensity plus hard work. A null result under that rule is not a direct test of Seiler’s three-zone polarized model against a pyramidal model.

**Direct threshold-heavy comparisons still matter, and they are small.** Neal et al. (2013): 12 male cyclists, randomized crossover, two 6-week periods separated by 4 weeks of detraining. Polarized was 6.4±1.4 h/week at 80/0/20% low/moderate/high; threshold was 7.5±2.0 h/week at 57/43/0%. Peak power rose 8±2% vs 3±1%, lactate threshold 9±3% vs 2±4%, and high-intensity capacity 85±14% vs 37±14% (all p<0.05 for the greater polarized change). Both periods improved 40 km time-trial performance; the abstract does not report a significantly greater 40 km gain after polarized training. Mitochondrial enzyme activities did not change. Volume was not perfectly matched. [Esteve-Lanao et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1519/r-19725.1): 12 sub-elite runners, 5 months, zone 3 matched (~8%), more zone 1 (80.5/11.8/8.3) beat more zone 2 (66.8/24.7/8.5) on a 10.4 km cross-country simulation (−157±13 s vs −121.5±7.1 s, p=0.03). [Muñoz et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2012-0350): recreational runners, 10 weeks; prescribed polarized vs between-threshold distributions both improved 10 km (about 5.0% vs 3.6%); the difference was not significant in the primary comparison.

**Contradictory or null head-to-heads.** [Treff et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2017.00515): 14 national-level male rowers, 11 weeks, zone 1 clamped near 93%. Polarized had more zone 3 than pyramidal (6±3% vs 2±1%) and a higher polarization index, but 2000 m power and VO₂max did not improve significantly in either group. [Filipas et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.14101): 60 well-trained male runners, 16 weeks; pyramidal, polarized, and both orders of switching all improved endurance markers. The pyramidal-then-polarized sequence had the largest gains (about 3% relative VO₂peak and 1.5% in a 5 km time trial). That is one running study, not a cyclist law that order of distributions is a large lever.

**2026 cyclist trial that is easy to misread.** [Moran-MacDonald et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-026-06380-w) (PMID 42560532; abstract only) randomized 19 moderately trained cyclists and triathletes (17 men) to 3 days/week of severe-domain HIIT for 6 weeks, with or without an extra 240 min/week of moderate-domain cycling. Both groups cut time-trial time by 8.5±1.2%. About 23 extra hours in the “polarized” arm produced no significant time×group interaction for time trial, peak work rate, LT1, or LT2. This compares HIIT alone with HIIT plus moderate-domain volume. It is **not** polarized vs threshold, not load-matched, and not evidence that easy volume is useless over a season or for durability. The abstract’s exploratory power statement (a between-group η² of 0.4 would need >100 participants per group) is internally hard to reconcile; full text was not available, so that statistic is not interpreted further.

**Practical implication.** For a trained amateur, keep most riding clearly easy and include some severe work. Do not reorganize the week to hit a polarized percentage, and do not conclude from 2024–2026 syntheses that threshold sessions are useless. Avoid making the heavy domain the bulk of a limited week; that narrower warning still has better support than “polarized beats pyramidal.”

**Uncertainty.** Female cyclists are scarce in the RCTs. Intervention lengths are often 4–12 weeks. Cove’s volume meta-regression (no detected association of weekly or total hours with VO₂max or time-trial change; VO₂max subset 20 studies/36 groups; time-trial subset 10/17) does **not** establish a safe volume optimum or a causal lack of benefit beyond a “necessary” volume. The paper’s weekly-hour summary for that subset is not repeated here: the reported dispersion is implausible for the stated range. A null regression in short, heterogeneous trials is not a finding that extra easy riding cannot help a 7–12 h cyclist over months. Volume conclusions belong in the volume brief.

<a id="tid-c03"></a>
### TID-C03 — Session-goal, time-in-zone, and numbered zone models are not interchangeable

**Classification:** Strong evidence that these are different measurements. The training effect of any one model is a separate claim (TID-C02).

**Support.** [Sylta, Tønnessen, and Seiler, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2013-0298) compared time-in-zone, session-goal, and a hybrid method on 570 sessions from 29 elite cross-country skiers (16 men, 13 women; VO₂max 76±7 mL·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹). The methods were not interchangeable; the paper supplies conversion factors rather than one true distribution. Seiler and Kjerland (2006) already showed that heart-rate time, session RPE, and lactate sampling can agree roughly while still not being the same construct. A high-intensity session contains substantial zone 1 time (warm-up, recoveries, cool-down). Session-goal polarization can therefore look pyramidal by time in zone.

**Zone models, using the platform glossary.**

| Model | What the numbers mean | What they are not |
|---|---|---|
| Three-zone research model | Zone 1 below LT1/VT1; zone 2 between first and second thresholds; zone 3 above the second threshold. Anchors must be stated (lactate, ventilatory, or a field surrogate). | Coggan zone 2, “Zone 2” on a cycling computer, or 56–75% FTP |
| Five- or six-zone heart-rate models | Coaching or laboratory bands, sometimes fixed %HRmax. Mateo-March et al. (2026) used 60–82%, 82–88%, and 89–100% HRmax as low/moderate/high for one analysis, and a separate six-band Edwards-style model for TRIMP. | Proof of three separately trainable physiological domains |
| Seven-zone cycling power model | A coaching convention, commonly percentages of FTP, from active recovery through neuromuscular. | Seven exclusive adaptive systems. Sweet spot is a coaching band, not a validated unique stimulus ([glossary](../CONTEXT.md)). |

**Practical implication.** When a plan is called polarized or pyramidal, state the method: session goal, heart-rate time, or power time, and the anchors. A week with two interval sessions and the rest easy can be session-goal polarized and time-in-zone pyramidal. Both descriptions can be true.

**Uncertainty.** Field critical power and a 70% of CP cut, as used by Mateo-March et al., are not laboratory LT1/LT2. Percentage-of-FTP zones drift when FTP is wrong.

<a id="tid-c04"></a>
### TID-C04 — Matched-load order of 4-, 8-, and 16-minute mesocycles has not been shown to matter

**Classification:** Moderate evidence for that specific claim in well-trained cyclists over 12 weeks. **Mostly coaching practice** that a traditional base–build–peak sequence, or the reverse, is required for amateurs.

**Candidate paper, not a confirmed transcript citation.** [Sylta et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27300278/) randomized 63 cyclists (VO₂peak 61.3±5.8 mL·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹) to 12 weeks with 24 interval sessions plus a high volume of low-intensity training. Increasing: 4×16 min (weeks 1–4), 4×8 (weeks 5–8), 4×4 (weeks 9–12), n=23. Decreasing: the opposite order, n=20. Mixed: intervals alternated. Mean session lactate was about 4.7, 9.2, and 12.7 mmol/L in the 4×16, 4×8, and 4×4 formats. Increasing therefore started with longer, lower-intensity intervals; decreasing started with shorter, higher-intensity intervals. Neither order is a pure threshold-then-VO₂ or VO₂-then-threshold sequence. Load was balanced. All groups improved about 5–10% in 40-minute mean power, peak power, and VO₂peak, with no significant group difference and a similar spread of individual responders. The authors concluded that mesocycle order, or mixing the formats, had little or no effect when overall load was the same.

Interval *format* comparisons, including acute time near VO₂max, are not repeated here. See [VO₂-focused intervals](vo2-intervals.md).

**Broader periodization.** [González-Ravé et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-022-00445-8) (11 studies, 200 athletes) did not find reverse periodization superior to traditional or block models for VO₂max or the performance outcomes they reviewed. Study quality was modest (mean PEDro 4.9). [Kiely, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-017-0823-y) and [Afonso et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1515/hukin-2017-0078) argue that much periodization doctrine outruns the trials: stress theory does not make a pre-drawn mesocycle biologically necessary, and many “periodization” studies are conceptually muddled. Those are critiques, not cycling RCTs.

**Practical implication.** Progress load and keep the work specific and recoverable. Do not spend planning effort on whether a 4-minute block must precede an 8-minute block. Keeping some severe work and some heavy-domain work in the same month is compatible with the evidence; they are overlapping stimuli (cardiac output, mitochondria, capillaries, buffering), not separate tanks that must be filled in order.

**Uncertainty.** Sylta’s cyclists were well trained and mostly the sample that completed a high low-intensity background. A short, under-recovered amateur block could still fail for fatigue reasons even if order is unimportant. One running study (Filipas et al., 2022) found a distribution *sequence* difference of small size; that does not overturn Sylta for interval-duration order.

<a id="tid-c05"></a>
### TID-C05 — HIT or moderate-intensity blocks can work; superiority over even spacing is not established

**Classification:** Moderate evidence that a short concentrated block, followed by easier days, can improve well-trained cyclists. **Plausible but uncertain** that block organization is superior to the same work spread across weeks. Conflicts are expected: samples are small and mostly male, and “block” is not one protocol.

**Support for a block effect.** [Rønnestad, Hansen, and Ellefsen, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0838.2012.01485.x): trained male cyclists, 4 weeks, similar HIT and LIT volume. Block (n=10, VO₂max 62±2): five HIT sessions in week 1, then one per week. Traditional (n=9, VO₂max 63±2): two HIT sessions per week. Block increased VO₂max 4.6±3.7%, peak power 2.1±2.8%, and power at 2 mmol/L 10±12%; traditional showed no significant changes. Between-group effect sizes were large to moderate. A 5-week elite skier comparison found a similar pattern ([Rønnestad et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.12418); n=10 vs 9). [Mølmen, Øfsteng, and Rønnestad, 2019](https://doi.org/10.2147/oajsm.s180408) meta-analyzed block vs traditional endurance organization and found small favorable effects for VO₂max (SMD 0.40, 95% CI 0.02 to 0.79) and peak power (SMD 0.28, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.54). That review closed in 2019.

**2022–2025 cyclist updates.** [Almquist et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2022.837634): 12 weeks, load-matched, trained cyclists (VO₂peak 58±8 mL·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹), block n=14 vs traditional n=16. No group difference in time-trial performance. Red-cell volume increased more with block (+10±16% relative difference, p=0.029); capillaries around type I fibers increased more with traditional (+20±12%, p=0.002). [Mølmen et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0000000000003706): randomized crossover, 30 well-trained male cyclists (VO₂max 70.5±4.6). Six moderate-intensity interval sessions in 7 days (5–7 × 10–14 min, RPE 14–15; achieved RPE 14.4±0.3, 66±5% of power at VO₂max, 85±3% HRmax, lactate 2.8±1.1 mmol/L) plus 6 days of active recovery beat time-matched regular preparation on power at 4 mmol/L (+4.0±4.4% vs −1.3±3.7%), power at VO₂max, and VO₂max, despite similar TRIMP. Fifteen-minute power was not significantly different. [Rønnestad et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsc.70067): crossover in well-trained cyclists (1 woman, 21 men; VO₂max 69.5±6.0). A moderate-intensity block and a short-interval HIT block both improved 15-minute power, with no difference (p=0.44). The moderate block improved power at 4 mmol/L more; the HIT block had a non-significant edge on 10-second sprint power. [Solli et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsc.12223): five short-interval HIT sessions in 6 days plus 6 easier days improved power at VO₂max more than regular training that already included four HIT sessions (3.7% vs 0.7%, p=0.009). VO₂max, power at 4 mmol/L, and 15-minute power did not differ significantly.

**Opposite result under a different “block”.** [Hebisz, Hebisz, and Drelak, 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph18168865): 20 trained mountain-bike cyclists, 8 weeks. Alternating 17-day low-intensity and 11-day sprint/HIIT blocks increased VO₂max less than simultaneous polarized organization (3.75±0.67 to 4.00±0.75 L/min vs 3.66±0.73 to 4.20±0.89 L/min). Peak power and threshold power rose similarly. This is not the Rønnestad microcycle.

**Practical implication.** A planned hard week (about five to six quality sessions) followed by several easier days is a reasonable occasional tool for a cyclist who already tolerates two hard sessions. It is not the default 7–12 h week. Judge it after the recovery days, not on the last day of the block. Do not treat “block,” “traditional,” and “polarized block” as synonyms.

**Uncertainty.** Superiority trials are short, male, and small. Almquist’s null 12-week result is the most direct warning against assuming blocks are generally better in trained cyclists. Illness and non-functional overreaching risk rises when hard sessions are stacked without a real unloading period ([Aubry et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0000000000000301); [Meeusen et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0b013e318279a10a)).

<a id="tid-c06"></a>
### TID-C06 — Two hard cycling sessions per week is a workable default, not a proven optimum

**Classification:** Moderate evidence that about two high-intensity endurance sessions per week, added to otherwise easier training, improves already trained cyclists over a few weeks. **Mostly coaching practice** that two is better than one or three for every trained amateur. Races and heavy strength are hard stress even when frequency trials ignore them.

**What the trials actually compare.**

| Pattern | What was shown | What was not shown |
|---|---|---|
| About 2 HIT sessions/week | [Laursen et al., 2002](https://doi.org/10.1097/00005768-200211000-00017): 38 cyclists and triathletes (VO₂peak 64.5±5.2), three different HIT formats twice weekly for 4 weeks plus usual low-intensity training. 40 km time trial improved about 4.4–5.8% and peak power 3.0–6.2% vs control. Seiler, Jøranson, Olesen, and Hetlelid (2013) also used two interval sessions per week in recreational cyclists; format ranking is in the interval brief. | Not a comparison with 1 or 3 sessions |
| 1 session/week, then 3 | [Billat et al., 1999](https://doi.org/10.1097/00005768-199901000-00024): 8 runners. Four weeks with one vVO₂max session/week improved velocity at VO₂max. The next 4 weeks with three such sessions did not further improve performance; end-test norepinephrine rose. Sequential, not parallel; not cyclists. | Not proof that 3 sessions are harmful in every context |
| 3 sessions/week | [Helgerud et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0b013e3180304570): moderately trained men, 3 days/week for 8 weeks; 4×4 and 15/15 raised VO₂max more than work-matched lower intensities. [Lenk et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.14814/phy2.70573): exploratory, 26 recreationally active adults (VO₂max 51.3±7.1), 6 weeks of 4×4 once, twice, or three times weekly. All frequencies improved VO₂max and time to exhaustion; the larger effects were in the twice- and thrice-weekly groups, with no clear extra benefit of three vs two. Wide confidence intervals. Not trained cyclists. | Not an optimum for 7–12 h trained cyclists |
| 3 sessions in a week, consecutive vs spaced | [Gross, Swensen, and King, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0b013e3180cac209): 15 trained cyclists, 3 weeks, three HIT sessions/week on consecutive days (n=9) or nonconsecutive days (n=6). Sessions were up to 8×2.5 min at peak aerobic power. Both schedules improved VO₂peak, peak power, and 5 km time-trial performance, with no between-group difference in the authors’ conclusion. | Not proof that next-day HIT is as recoverable over a season |
| 4 shorter vs 2 longer, volume matched | [Tønnessen, Hisdal, and Rønnestad, 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph17093190): elite skiers and biathletes, intervals at ~85% HRmax. Two longer sessions (n=9) improved economy and fractional use at anaerobic threshold; four shorter sessions (n=6) did not show those changes. Neither group changed VO₂max significantly. Authors suggest the longer gap may have helped. | ~85% HRmax is not automatically severe-domain VO₂ work. Skiers, tiny sample, one woman per group |
| Short blocks of 5–6 | See TID-C05. Tolerable for about a week in well-trained cyclists if easier days follow. | Not a year-round frequency |

**Counting hard work.** A hard cycling session, in this platform’s glossary, is a session with a substantial high-intensity, threshold, or race stimulus. Two hard days does **not** mean two VO₂ sessions plus a threshold session plus a race. Strength sessions with real load are an additional neuromuscular stress; programming them is owned by the strength brief, but they occupy recovery capacity. Frequency trials almost never jointly optimize races, threshold, VO₂ work, and lifting. Until they do, the conservative rule is to count each of those as hard and protect the quality of the sessions that match the current goal.

**Practical implication.** Default to two hard cycling exposures in a 7–12 h week: one more severe, one heavy-domain or race-specific, separated so the second is still high quality. A third is optional and conditional, not a target. One hard session per week can maintain or slowly build in a constrained week; it is unlikely to be the fastest way to raise VO₂max if recovery would allow a second. More than three hard cycling sessions in an ordinary week is a block or a race week, not a habit.

**Uncertainty.** There is no retrieved factorial trial of 1 vs 2 vs 3 vs >3 severe sessions per week in trained cyclists at matched weekly hours. “Two” is the frequency most often *used* in successful interventions, which is why it became coaching default. That history is not an optimization experiment.

<a id="tid-c07"></a>
### TID-C07 — About 48 hours is a reasonable gap after severe work; it is not a recovery law

**Classification:** Moderate evidence that cardiac parasympathetic recovery is slower after threshold and high-intensity exercise than after easy exercise, often on the order of 24–48 h or longer after severe work ([Stanley, Peake, and Buchheit, 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-013-0083-4)). **Plausible but uncertain** that performance adaptation is maximized at exactly 48 rather than 24 or 72 h. Easy riding below the first threshold has a low *autonomic* cost for up to about 2 hours in highly trained athletes; that does not make multi-hour rides free.

**Support.** Stanley et al. (2013), a review rather than a new trial, summarized complete cardiac autonomic recovery as up to 24 h after low-intensity exercise, 24–48 h after threshold-intensity exercise, and at least 48 h after high-intensity exercise. Duration was not clearly the dominant factor in the limited data, and fitter athletes recovered faster. This is a marker, not the next session’s power. [Seiler, Haugen, and Kuffel, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0b013e318060f17d): highly trained male runners (n=9, VO₂max 72±5, 14±3 h/week) regained heart-rate variability within 5–10 minutes after 60 or 120 minutes below VT1. Sixty minutes with 30 minutes between VT1 and VT2 (lactate 2.7±0.4 mmol/L), or 6×3 min at 96% VO₂max, delayed recovery similarly, with return by about 30 minutes in that highly trained group. Trained runners (n=8, VO₂max 60±5, 7±1 h/week) needed at least 90 minutes after the same interval session. Those are hour-scale autonomic traces, not next-day performance.

Gross et al. (2007) is the closest cyclist scheduling trial: three HIT sessions on consecutive days did not produce a different group-mean adaptation from nonconsecutive days over 3 weeks. The sample (15) cannot establish equivalence, and a 3-week mean can hide athletes who should have stopped.

**Active recovery vs rest vs easy endurance.** [Ortiz et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000002589) found weak evidence that post-exercise active-recovery *techniques* improve subsequent performance in competitive athletes; psychological benefit is more plausible than a physiological requirement. That review is not a test of a 90-minute endurance ride. Within-interval active vs passive recovery is a different question and is not answered here. Complete rest removes training stress and also removes a low-intensity stimulus. For a cyclist whose limit is recovery of the next hard session, rest or a short easy ride are both acceptable. The choice is not settled by a performance RCT in trained cyclists.

**Multi-hour rides are training.** Seiler et al. (2007) supports ≤120 minutes below VT1 as a small autonomic disturbance in athletes already training ~14 h/week. It does not measure glycogen, muscle damage, or next-day interval quality after 4 hours, and the less-trained subgroup recovered more slowly from hard work. A 4-hour ride at low relative intensity still costs time, carbohydrate, and musculoskeletal load. If the next hard session is worse because of it, the ride was not “recovery.”

**Practical implication.** Place severe sessions about 48 h apart when the goal is repeatable quality. Use 24 h spacing only inside a short, intentional block with easier days afterward. If 72 h is what keeps the second session honest, that is not a scientific failure. Keep intervening rides below the first threshold and short enough that the next quality session is intact. Do not copy a professional 4-hour “recovery” ride into a 7–12 h week.

**Uncertainty.** No retrieved randomized endurance trial isolates 24 vs 48 vs 72 h between otherwise identical HIT sessions and follows performance for more than a few weeks. Autonomic reviews and short scheduling trials answer different questions.

<a id="tid-c08"></a>
### TID-C08 — Do not prescribe a universal maintenance minimum or an obligatory mathematical deload

**Classification:** Moderate evidence that a pre-competition taper which cuts volume and keeps intensity can improve performance in competitive athletes ([Bosquet et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0b013e31806010e0); [Wang et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0282838)). **Mostly coaching practice** that every fourth week must be unloaded by a fixed percentage. **Plausible but uncertain** that any single weekly frequency maintains VO₂max and threshold in this audience. Intensity seems harder to cut than frequency or duration in non-elite maintenance studies; those studies are not trained cyclists in-season.

**Maintenance, and why it does not transfer as a number.**

| Source | Population and design | What held, and for how long | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Hickson and Rosenkoetter, 1981](https://doi.org/10.1249/00005768-198101000-00011) | Young adults after 10 weeks of 6 days/week training; then 2 or 4 days/week for 15 weeks, intensity and duration kept | VO₂max maintained at both reduced frequencies | Recently trained non-athletes |
| [Hickson et al., 1982](https://doi.org/10.1152/jappl.1982.53.1.225) | Same model; duration cut to 26 or 13 min/day, intensity and frequency kept | VO₂max and short-term endurance maintained 15 weeks | Same population |
| [Hickson et al., 1985](https://doi.org/10.1152/jappl.1985.58.2.492) | Intensity cut by one-third or two-thirds; frequency and duration kept | VO₂max gains were not maintained; larger intensity cut, larger loss | Intensity, not a cyclist’s “zone 2,” was the experimental variable |
| [Coyle et al., 1984](https://doi.org/10.1152/jappl.1984.57.6.1857) | 7 endurance-trained subjects, complete stop | VO₂max −7% by 21 days, −16% by 56 days; muscle enzyme half-time ~12 days; capillaries did not decline over the observed period | Cessation, not reduced training |
| [Neufer et al., 1987](https://doi.org/10.1249/00005768-198604001-00382) | Competitive male swimmers, 4 weeks | 3 sessions/week maintained VO₂max better than 1 session/week or rest; swim power fell in all groups | Swimming, 4 weeks |
| [Rønnestad, Askestad, and Hansen, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-014-2919-5) | Well-trained cyclists, 8-week transition | One HIT session every 7–10 days plus low-intensity training (n=7) vs low-intensity emphasis (n=6). Magnitude-based inference favored the HIT group for power at 4 mmol/L and a 40-minute trial | n=13, not a test of every-14-days vs twice weekly |
| [Spiering et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000003964) | Narrative review, general and military populations | Endurance performance maintained up to 15 weeks with frequency as low as 2 sessions/week, or volume cut 33–66%, if intensity is maintained | Explicitly not a trained-cyclist prescription |

A maintenance minimum depends on training status, which quality (VO₂max, threshold, durability, strength), and how long the reduction lasts. Copying “one HIT every 10 days” or “two 13-minute sessions” as a universal floor would over-read these papers.

**Deload vs taper.** Bosquet et al. (2007) included 27 taper datasets in competitive athletes. The subgroup pattern associated with larger effects was about 2 weeks, exponential volume reduction of roughly 41–60% (overall effect 0.72±0.36), without cutting intensity or frequency. Those ranges come from comparing heterogeneous studies, not from a factorial optimum. Wang et al. (2023) included 14 studies; time-trial SMD was −0.45 after tapering. Volume cuts of 41–60%, maintained intensity and frequency, and tapers up to 21 days were associated with better time trials. Pre-taper overload looked better than tapering without it in that meta-analysis. [Aubry et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0000000000000301) is the conflict: among 33 trained male triathletes, athletes who were only acutely fatigued after an overload supercompensated more than those who became functionally overreached (about 2.6±1.1% greater peak performance), and infections were more common in the overreached group (70% vs 20%). “Overload then deload” is not automatically the path to a peak. A routine 3:1 mathematical unload is tradition ([Issurin, 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18212712/)), not an endurance RCT result.

**Recoverability-guided placement.** [Javaloyes et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2018-0122): 17 well-trained cyclists, 8 weeks. Heart-rate-variability-guided training improved peak power 5.1%, power at VT2 13.9%, and a 40-minute time trial 7.3%; traditional periodization did not improve significantly. Between-group tests were not significant; magnitude-based inference suggested a likely benefit. [Javaloyes et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000003337): 20 well-trained cyclists; HRV-guided training improved VO₂max, peak power, both ventilatory-threshold powers, and a 40-minute time trial, while block periodization improved power at VT2. [Vesterinen et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0000000000000910): recreational runners; HRV guidance used fewer moderate/HIT sessions (13.2±6.0 vs 17.7±2.5) and improved 3000 m running, with a small between-group difference. These trials support adjusting hard days to recovery signals. They do not require a specific HRV device, and they do not prove morning HRV beats simply moving a session when the legs and the last workout say so.

**Practical implication.** Insert easier days when quality, sleep, mood, or illness says the load is accumulating. Before a target event, reduce volume for about 7–14 days and keep some intensity. Do not schedule a fixed 40% deload every fourth week as if physiology required the arithmetic. If the goal of an easy week is to *hold* fitness, keep a short quality exposure rather than making the whole week soft — that inference comes mainly from non-elite intensity-reduction studies and should be applied cautiously.

**Uncertainty.** Taper meta-analyses mix sports and performance tests. HRV trials are small. No study defines the deload percentage that maximizes a 12-week amateur cycling gain.

<a id="tid-c09"></a>
### TID-C09 — Distribution should change with volume, days, phase, and event, but the percentages are not experimentally fixed

**Classification:** Moderate evidence that one elite template should not be copied across 8 h and 25 h weeks (observational volume and intensity differences; recovery physiology in TID-C07). **Plausible but uncertain** for any specific zone percentage by phase or event in trained amateur cyclists. Factorial cyclist RCTs of those modifiers were not found.

**Support.** Norwegian Olympic endurance coaches described high low-intensity volume plus 2–3 weekly “key workout” days that could contain several intensive sessions, with large sport-specific differences ([Sandbakk et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-025-00848-3)). World-class runners keep ≥80% of running volume at low intensity across the year, then increase race-pace work toward the main competition ([Haugen et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01692-1)). Highly trained and elite runners in a systematic review more often looked pyramidal; marathoners tended more pyramidal and 1500 m runners more polarized ([Casado et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2021-0435)). [Stöggl and Sperlich, 2015](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2015.00295) already noted that retrospective studies often show pyramidal distributions, with polarized patterns in some phases, while early RCTs favored polarized over threshold or high-volume low-intensity emphasis in less elite groups. That 2015 reading is updated by TID-C02: the RCT advantage has not generalized into a time-trial winner.

Professional road cyclists do not train like a polarized laboratory arm. Mateo-March et al. (2026) full text: 16 women and 16 men, one WorldTour team, October–December 2018, 10 weeks before the first race of 2019. About 6 sessions/week. Women 16.7±2.6 h/week, men 19.1±2.7 h/week. Power zones used estimated critical power as the moderate/high boundary and an **assumed** 70% of CP as the low/moderate boundary, not a measured LT1. More than 90% of riders were pyramidal (low > moderate > high). Polarization index >2.0 was required to call a distribution polarized; group means were about 1.03–1.07 by power and 1.54–1.71 by heart rate. Power-time distribution: women 59.7±10.4 / 32.8±9.0 / 7.4±3.8%; men 70.4±6.2 / 25.4±5.1 / 4.2±1.8%. Heart-rate time was also pyramidal, with more high-intensity time than power time. The last week dropped to 11.2±4.6 h in women and 17.7±4.9 h in men. [van Erp, Sanders, and de Koning, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2019-0320) (abstract): 4-year retrospective, 20 men and 10 women. Men accumulated more low-intensity time; women had higher load per kilometre and appeared to compensate for lower volume with higher intensity. TSS differences were trivial. Zone percentages were not in the abstract.

**Practical implication.** At 7–12 h and 4–6 days, protect two quality sessions and fill the rest with easy riding you can repeat. A lower percentage of zone 1 than a WorldTour man is an arithmetic consequence of fewer hours, not a mistake. Shift emphasis toward event-specific intensity only as the event approaches, and only if the easy background is still recoverable. Short, high-neuromuscular events justify more severe work than a long road race; that is specificity, not a retrieved cyclist RCT of polarized vs pyramidal by event.

**Uncertainty.** Professional data are one team or small cohorts, often men, and pre-season or in-season observational. They do not test whether those riders would have been faster with a different distribution.

<a id="labels"></a>
## Labels and the 80/20 problem

The prompt asks for polarized, pyramidal, threshold-heavy, high-volume low-intensity, and mixed training. In the three-zone research model:

- **Polarized:** zone 1 > zone 3 > zone 2, often caricatured as ~80/0–5/15–20. The useful content is “little work *between* thresholds, some work *above* the second threshold, most work easy.”
- **Pyramidal:** zone 1 > zone 2 > zone 3. Most elite observational series look like this when time, not session goal, is counted ([Stöggl and Sperlich, 2015](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2015.00295); Mateo-March et al., 2026).
- **Threshold-heavy:** a large share in zone 2, sometimes with little zone 3 (Neal’s threshold arm was 57/43/0). This is the distribution most often beaten in small direct trials, not the distribution of every “Norwegian” threshold session.
- **High-volume low-intensity:** emphasis on zone 1 with little or no severe work. It can improve less-trained people and is part of every successful high-volume program, but replacing hard sessions with only easy volume has not matched programs that keep some HIT in trained athletes (Laursen et al., 2002; Rønnestad transition data). Moran-MacDonald et al. (2026) shows the opposite short-term pattern in moderately trained athletes: extra moderate-domain hours on top of three HIIT days did not add a detectable 6-week time-trial gain. Both results can stand. HIT is a potent short-term stimulus; low-intensity volume is not thereby proven irrelevant over longer horizons or for durability.
- **Mixed / traditional:** some easy, some threshold, some severe work in the same weeks. Much “traditional” periodization in the cyclist trials is simply two HIT sessions per week plus low-intensity riding, not a pure threshold diet.

Intensities do not adapt exclusive systems. Severe intervals, threshold intervals, and easy endurance all load oxygen delivery and muscle oxidative capacity, with different fatigue costs and different emphasis (more cardiac and neuromuscular strain at the top end; more time-at-intensity and substrate turnover in the heavy and moderate domains). Choosing a distribution is a recoverability and specificity decision, not a choice of which single physiological “system” to train.

### Why amateurs misread 80/20

1. **Session goal vs time.** Two hard sessions and five easy sessions in a seven-session week is about 71/29 by session count, not 80/20. Exact 80/20 by session count is two hard sessions and eight easy sessions (2 of 10). The same week can still be mostly zone 1 by time in zone, because warm-ups and recoveries are easy. Do not force either percentage.
2. **Zone 2 collision.** Research zone 2 is between thresholds. Cycling-software zone 2 is often a low-intensity band inside research zone 1. Training “80% in zone 2” on a bike computer is not Seiler’s prescription. See the glossary and the easy-intensity brief.
3. **Volume dependence.** Eighty percent of 25 h is 20 h easy. Eighty percent of 8 h is 6.4 h easy and leaves little room for two full quality sessions plus warm-ups. The principle survives; the percentage should not be forced.
4. **Cove’s label is a third meaning.** Intervals under 5 minutes plus some easy riding were “polarized” in that meta-analysis even without a measured zone distribution. Do not cite Cove as an 80/20 trial.

<a id="comparative"></a>
## Comparative evidence

### Evidence capsules

**Neal et al., 2013 — trained male cyclists, polarized vs threshold.** Randomized crossover, n=12, 6+6 weeks. Best direct cyclist comparison of a low/high split against a large between-threshold share. Polarized favored peak power, lactate threshold, and a high-intensity capacity test. 40 km time trial improved in both arms; a superior 40 km effect is not stated in the abstract. Volume slightly higher in the threshold arm. Abstract only.

**Stöggl and Sperlich, 2014 — mixed endurance athletes.** Forty-eight runners, cyclists, triathletes, and skiers (VO₂peak 62.6±7.1), 9 weeks, four concepts: high volume, threshold, HIIT, polarized. The abstract reports the largest VO₂peak rise in polarized training (+6.8 mL·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹, 11.7%) and concludes polarized produced the greatest improvements in most key endurance variables. Between-group statistics were incompletely preserved in the retrieved abstract. Not cyclists only. This paper anchors the early “polarized wins” narrative and should be read with Treff, Filipas, and the 2024–2025 syntheses.

**Rosenblat et al., 2019 — polarized vs threshold meta-analysis.** Small, low PEDro scores, moderate time-trial effect favoring polarized. It does not include the later pyramidal comparisons. Still the cleanest pooled contrast against threshold-heavy models, and still too thin to set an amateur percentage.

**Silva Oliveira et al., 2024 — polarized vs other distributions.** Full-text results retrieved. Small VO₂peak benefit, null time-trial benefit, benefit more visible under 12 weeks and in highly trained subgroups. Authors rated certainty for the VO₂peak estimate high; risk of bias for randomized trials was “some concerns.” A small SMD whose confidence interval nearly includes zero is not a large programming lever.

**Rosenblat et al., 2025 — individual-participant network meta-analysis.** Best available polarized vs pyramidal comparison. No overall VO₂max or time-trial difference. Performance level may modify the VO₂max response. Abstract only. Endurance athletes, not a cyclist-only sample. “No difference” is not equivalence.

**Cove et al., 2025 — cyclist-only multilevel meta-analysis.** Full text retrieved (open access). Training works. Their polarized vs non-polarized split does not separate outcomes. Classification is an interval-duration rule, not an 80/20 or below-LT1 test. Inclusion was a sex-specific study-mean Tier 3 threshold, not every rider at or above 59 mL·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹. Search closed 23 October 2023. Certainty is low, and the null volume regression must not be read as “extra hours cannot help.” Between-model differences were not significant. Abstract and body disagree on study counts and confidence intervals, so only the abstract point estimates are used (g=0.42 for VO₂max and g=0.39 for time trial; 41 studies, 797 participants). The paper’s practical-implications paragraph still floats a possible VO₂max benefit for their polarized category; the statistical contrast does not confirm it.

**Rivera-Köfler et al., 2025 — scoping review.** Fifteen studies. Polarized and pyramidal models were both associated with VO₂max and aerobic-threshold improvements; all models they included could improve anaerobic-threshold speed or power. Not a meta-analysis. Abstract only. Consistent with “no universal winner.”

**Nøst, Aune, and van den Tillaar, 2024 — systematic review of polarized training and VO₂max/economy.** Fourteen studies. Concludes that a polarized pattern (they restate about 75–80% low and 15–20% high) can raise VO₂max and economy in the short term. This is closer to a narrative endorsement of the pattern than a head-to-head effect size. Conclusion taken from the abstract.

**Treff et al., 2017 and Filipas et al., 2022.** Elite rowers: polarized not superior to pyramidal when zone 1 is already ~93%. Well-trained male runners: all four periodizations worked; switching from pyramidal to polarized had a small edge. Neither is a cyclist prescription.

**Moran-MacDonald et al., 2026.** HIIT three times weekly ± 4 h/week moderate-domain cycling, 6 weeks, moderately trained, mostly men. Short-term time-trial gains tracked the severe work. Do not cite as polarized vs pyramidal or as a season-long volume trial.

**Mateo-March et al., 2026.** Observational WorldTour pre-season. Pyramidal by time in zone. Women: less volume, higher intensity fraction. Zone boundaries were field estimates. Describes practice; does not prove optimality.

<a id="modifiers"></a>
### What should change the mix

| Modifier | Evidence-backed direction | Over-precise claim to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Weekly hours | More hours force a larger easy share if hard-session count stays near two, because hard-session recovery does not scale linearly with available time (TID-C07; Seiler, 2010). | “At 8 h you must be 80/20; at 12 h you must be 90/10.” |
| Experience | Less-trained people improve from many distributions, including three moderate or HIT sessions per week (Helgerud et al., 2007; Lenk et al., 2025). Already trained cyclists need hard work to be added to, not substituted for, a recoverable background (Laursen et al., 2002), and may show smaller distribution differences (Cove et al., 2025). Rosenblat et al. (2025) suggest competitive athletes might favor polarized and recreational athletes pyramidal for VO₂max. That subgroup is hypothesis-generating. | “Trained cyclists must polarize; beginners must not.” |
| Phase | Observational: race-specific intensity rises toward competition; tapers cut volume (Haugen et al., 2022; Mateo-March last-week hours). Experimental: interval-duration order across 12 weeks did not matter (Sylta et al., 2016). | A mandatory base, build, and peak sequence. |
| Event | Specificity argument: more severe and neuromuscular work for short events; more durable sub-threshold work for long road races. Supported by elite running TID differences (Casado et al., 2022), not by a cyclist RCT. | A different optimal percentage for every event distance. |
| Available days | Four days can hold two hard sessions and two easy rides. Six days should not automatically become four hard sessions. Sandbakk et al. (2025) “3–5 intensive sessions” on 2–3 key days describes double-session elite environments, not five HIT days for an amateur. | Matching a coach interview’s session count. |

<a id="periodization"></a>
## Periodization and sequencing

### Models, and how strong the endurance evidence is

| Model | Usual meaning | Best available evidence | Grade of “this is superior” |
|---|---|---|---|
| Traditional linear / base–build–peak | Volume first, then threshold, then higher intensity and race specificity, often with planned unload weeks | Common in observational elite years (Haugen et al., 2022). Head-to-head endurance RCTs are scarce and confounded. García-Pallarés et al. (2010): 10 world-class kayakers, two seasons, not a randomized parallel trial. Block was 10 weeks and 120 h shorter yet produced similar VO₂peak gains (about 8–11%) and larger peak-speed gains. | Mostly coaching practice as a required sequence |
| Reverse | High intensity first, then more volume or specificity | González-Ravé et al. (2022): not superior overall | Not supported as superior |
| Even / mixed weeks | Two HIT or one HIT plus one threshold session most weeks, plus easy riding | The control arm in several cyclist trials; often improves, sometimes less than a block over 4–5 weeks, not over 12 weeks (Almquist et al., 2022) | Moderate evidence that it works; not proven inferior in general |
| HIT block | Several HIT sessions in a few days, then fewer | Rønnestad et al. 2014 and 2016; Solli et al. 2025 | Plausible short-term benefit; not a default |
| Moderate-intensity / “threshold” block | Several heavy-domain interval sessions in a week, then recovery | Mølmen et al. 2025; Rønnestad et al. 2025 | Moderate evidence it can raise threshold markers in well-trained male cyclists; not evidence to make threshold the whole year |
| Polarized block | Emphasis on low plus severe, sometimes concentrated | Hebisz et al. 2021 used a different block contrast and found simultaneous polarized organization better for VO₂max than alternating LIT and SIT/HIIT blocks | Too heterogeneous to rank |
| Load-matched interval-order periodization | Same sessions, different mesocycle order | Sylta et al. 2016 | Moderate evidence order is a small lever |

Issurin (2008) argued that traditional mixed training gives conflicting stimuli and that concentrated blocks fit better. That is a theoretical review. The cyclist trials above are the empirical check, and they do not unanimously agree.

### Should VO₂ work precede threshold work?

The question is real. Sylta et al. (2016) does not answer it as an exclusive-system order. Neither arm isolated a pure threshold phase or a pure VO₂ phase, and neither order had a 12-week advantage when load was matched ([Sylta et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27300278/)).

1. **Longer, lower-intensity intervals first (Sylta increasing).** 4×16, then 4×8, then 4×4, with mean session lactate about 4.7, then 9.2, then 12.7 mmol/L. That is not “VO₂ work first.” It was not better than the reverse or the mix.
2. **Shorter, higher-intensity intervals first (Sylta decreasing).** The opposite order. That is not “threshold work first,” and it is not a reverse-periodization proof. It also had no 12-week advantage.
3. **Both maintained.** This matches successful observational practice (some severe work and some threshold or race-pace work across phases; Haugen et al., 2022; Sandbakk et al., 2025) and matches the physiology: the stimuli overlap. A concentrated moderate-intensity week can still help in general preparation (Mølmen et al., 2025) without having to precede all severe work. Rønnestad et al. (2025) show that either a moderate block or a HIT block can move well-trained cyclists; they do not show that one must precede the other. Reverse periodization, as a named model, was not superior in the González-Ravé review.

**Maintenance dose.** See TID-C08. For a trained cyclist leaving a build, the least speculative statement is: do not drop all severe and heavy work for many weeks if the goal is to hold power at threshold and VO₂-related fitness, and do not invent a universal minimum. One HIT exposure every 7–10 days helped a tiny sample through an 8-week transition better than low-intensity emphasis (Rønnestad, Askestad, and Hansen, 2014). That is a candidate floor for a transition, not a prescription for in-season amateurs, women, or durability.

<a id="frequency"></a>
## Hard-session frequency

The question is the balance of adaptation and recovery at 1, 2, 3, and occasionally more than 3 hard sessions per week, with VO₂ sessions, threshold sessions, races, and strength counted correctly.

**VO₂ / severe sessions.** Two per week is the best-documented *effective* dose in highly trained cyclists over about 4 weeks (Laursen et al., 2002), not the proven maximum. Three per week can raise VO₂max in moderately trained men (Helgerud et al., 2007) and was not clearly better than two in recreational adults doing 4×4 (Lenk et al., 2025). Jumping from one to three vVO₂max sessions did not add performance in Billat et al. (1999) and perturbed a stress marker. Five in a week can be a block if recovery follows (TID-C05).

**Threshold / heavy-domain sessions.** These are hard sessions, not “medium days” that can be added freely beside two VO₂ sessions. A week of six moderate-intensity interval sessions improved well-trained cyclists only as a block with a recovery week (Mølmen et al., 2025). Tønnessen et al. (2020) suggest that splitting threshold-ish work (~85% HRmax) into four short sessions was less effective than two longer ones in elite skiers, possibly because recovery between stimuli was worse. That is a reason not to pepper the week with extra threshold, not a reason to fear a single well-placed threshold session.

**Races.** A race replaces a planned hard session. It is not an extra quality day on top of two interval sessions unless the athlete has demonstrated that they can absorb it. The days after a long race are not automatically easy just because power was “zone 2” on the file; duration and surges matter (TID-C07).

**Strength.** Heavy strength is not a VO₂ session and should not be counted as one. It still competes for recovery, especially leg pressing strength near a severe cycling day. The strength brief owns the interference evidence. Until a trial combines them, do not schedule the week’s heaviest lifting, a VO₂ session, a threshold session, and a race as four independent “adaptations.”

**Is two experimentally optimal?** No. It is the modal successful experimental dose and the coaching default that follows from autonomic recovery estimates (Stanley et al., 2013) plus the practical limit of amateur weeks. Optimal would require a dose–response trial at fixed weekly hours. That trial was not found.

<a id="recovery"></a>
## Recovery between hard sessions

### 24 vs 48 vs 72 hours

| Gap | Evidence | Use |
|---|---|---|
| ~24 h | Possible inside a 5–6 session microcycle in well-trained cyclists, or in Gross et al. (2007) for 3 weeks of HIT. Autonomic reviews do not describe high-intensity exercise as fully recovered at 24 h (Stanley et al., 2013). | Short blocks only, with a subsequent easy period. Not the default gap. |
| ~48 h | Matches the “at least 48 h after high intensity” autonomic summary and the spacing implied by two sessions per week. Not proven superior to 72 h for adaptation. | Default when the goal is two high-quality severe or threshold sessions. |
| ~72 h | Not tested as an inferior dose. May be necessary when sessions are long, novel, or stacked with strength, poor sleep, or low carbohydrate availability (fueling brief). | Acceptable, especially at 7 h/week or under life stress. |

Heart-rate variability returning is not the same as restored interval power. Use the next session’s ability to hit the intended work as the performance check. Persistent failure, elevated soreness, or mood disturbance is a reason to insert easy days, not a reason to add intensity to “break through.” The overtraining consensus stresses prolonged maladaptation and the need to exclude illness, low energy availability, and iron deficiency before labeling overtraining syndrome (Meeusen et al., 2013). This brief is not a diagnostic tool.

### Rest, spinning, and easy endurance

- **Complete rest** removes load. It is appropriate when ill, when a session was extremely damaging, or when life stress is already maximal. There is no cyclist RCT showing that an easy ride is required between hard days for greater adaptation.
- **Short easy riding** (well below LT1/VT1, typically under about 2 hours) is the closest experimental analogue to “low autonomic cost” (Seiler et al., 2007, highly trained runners). It can maintain some aerobic stimulus and is the reasonable default between hard days in a 7–12 h week.
- **Active recovery as a modality** (spinning immediately after a hard session, light movement the same day) has weak performance evidence (Ortiz et al., 2019). It may feel better. It is not a required dose.
- **Multi-hour “recovery” rides** are low-intensity *training*. Professionals can place 3–5 h below the first threshold between hard sessions because their absolute fitness, recovery environment, and years of volume make the relative cost smaller. Mateo-March’s WorldTour men still accumulated ~19 h/week with only ~6 sessions, so easy days were long. That duration is a large fraction of an amateur’s entire week. Transferring it will often steal the next quality session or push total load past what 7–12 h of life stress can absorb.

If an easy day creeps above the first threshold, it becomes a third hard stimulus. That is the usual way “recovery rides” impair recovery. Prescribe them by conversation pace, ventilatory comfort, and a ceiling below estimated LT1, not by a heroic duration. Heart rate, power, and RPE together are safer than any single number; the easy-intensity brief owns the prescription details.

### Deloads without obligatory arithmetic

A deload is justified when it restores the ability to do specific, progressive work or when it is a pre-event taper. It is not justified because a spreadsheet reached week 4.

- **Before an event:** reduce volume substantially for about 1–2 weeks, keep intensity touches, do not add new session types (Bosquet et al., 2007; Wang et al., 2023). Do not deliberately drive functional overreaching in the hope of a bigger rebound (Aubry et al., 2014).
- **During a build:** if sessions are completed and the athlete is healthy, a mathematical unload is optional. If sessions are being cut short, resting heart rate or mood is off, or illness is appearing, reduce volume for several days to a week and keep at most one short quality session if the athlete is well.
- **After a HIT or moderate block:** the trials that showed benefit tested after about 6 easier days (Mølmen et al., 2025; Rønnestad et al., 2025; Solli et al., 2025). The recovery period is part of the intervention, not an optional extra.

<a id="transfer"></a>
## 7–12 h versus 20–30 h

| Decision | 7–12 h trained amateur | 20–30 h professional or elite endurance athlete |
|---|---|---|
| Easy share | Enough easy riding to separate two quality sessions. Percentage of zone 1 will often be lower than 80% of time because the denominator is small. | Long easy rides are how weekly hours exist. Time-in-zone often looks pyramidal (Mateo-March et al., 2026). Session goals can still be mostly easy. |
| Hard frequency | Default two. Third only if the first two are high quality. | 2–3 key days, sometimes more than one intensive session on a key day (Sandbakk et al., 2025). Not five amateur HIT days. |
| Threshold | One heavy-domain session can be the second quality day. It should not fill the other days. | Race terrain and group riding create moderate-intensity time even when the intent is easy. That is not a reason for an amateur to add sweet-spot volume. |
| Long ride | A long ride is a training stress. Cap it so the next hard session survives. It is not a 4-hour recovery ride. | Multi-hour easy rides are normal and still not “free,” but relative intensity and recovery support differ. |
| Blocks | Optional, rare, followed by easier days. | Used by some elite programs; still not universally superior (Almquist et al., 2022). |
| Deload | Recoverability-guided, plus a real taper before events. | Also individual; last pre-competition week in Mateo-March still differed by sex and was not a 40% formula. |

Women in the professional series trained fewer hours and a higher intensity fraction than men (Mateo-March et al., 2026; van Erp et al., 2020). Do not hand a male WorldTour low-intensity percentage to a female amateur, or the reverse. Most intervention trials cited here are male. That is a standing limit on every “optimal” claim.

<a id="practice"></a>
## Conservative placement for 7–12 h

These are applications of the conclusions, not extra experimental optima.

**Ordinary week**

- Two hard cycling sessions, about 48 h apart, or 72 h if the second session would otherwise degrade.
- One of them severe (VO₂-oriented). One of them heavy-domain or race-specific, not a second all-out VO₂ session by default.
- Remaining rides easy, below the first threshold, mostly 45–120 minutes. One longer easy ride is reasonable at 10–12 h if it does not spoil the next hard session. At 7 h, the long ride should not become a third hard day by duration plus hills.
- Strength, if used, is not placed as a third invisible hard day beside both quality rides. Prefer a gap from the more severe session. Details: strength brief.
- A race replaces one hard session. Do not add intervals the day before or the day after unless recovery is obviously complete.

**What not to do in the name of optimization**

- Hit 80.0% zone 1 on a seven-zone file.
- Fill non-interval days with sweet spot because the week “does not look polarized enough.”
- Sequence 4-, 8-, and 16-minute blocks as if order were the adaptation (Sylta et al., 2016).
- Run a five-session HIT block without the following easy days.
- Take a fixed 40% volume cut every fourth week while healthy and progressing, or, conversely, never reduce load when sessions are failing.
- Treat a 4-hour professional recovery ride as the amateur easy day.

**When to deviate**

- A short specific block (one week of extra threshold *or* extra HIT, not both piled on) if the athlete has been completing two hard sessions and the event is close enough to use the fitness. Retest or judge after the easy days.
- One hard session only, during travel, illness recovery, or a maintenance week. Expect holding, not rapid gains (TID-C08).
- Three quality exposures in a high-recovery week at 12 h, only if at least one is clearly less severe than a VO₂ session and the others remain high quality for more than a single week.

**Safety.** Stop hard training with fever, chest pain, or an unexplained collapse in performance. Persistent fatigue with mood change, recurrent illness, or suspected low energy availability needs assessment beyond a training-plan tweak (Meeusen et al., 2013; energy-availability brief). Do not use a deload formula as medical care.

## Open questions

1. A load-matched, sex-balanced trial of polarized vs pyramidal vs a modest threshold share in trained cyclists at 7–12 h/week, over at least 16 weeks, with durability and time-trial outcomes — not only VO₂peak.
2. A true dose–response of 1 vs 2 vs 3 severe sessions per week at fixed weekly hours, counting threshold and strength as co-exposures.
3. 24 vs 48 vs 72 h spacing with next-session power as the outcome, over more than 3 weeks.
4. Whether multi-hour rides below LT1 improve durability more than the same hours split, once hard-session quality is protected. That question sits with the volume and long-ride briefs; it is not answered by Seiler et al. (2007).
5. Maintenance minima for already trained cyclists, by sex, for VO₂max vs power at LT2 vs durability, over 4–12 weeks.
6. Whether HRV-guided placement beats simple session-quality rules once both are applied consistently. Current cyclist trials are small and use magnitude-based inference alongside or instead of conventional tests.
7. Reconciliation of Cove’s interval-duration classification with a polarization-index classification in the same cyclist dataset. Until that exists, “cyclist meta-analysis shows polarized is not better” and “polarized beats threshold” can both be narrowly true.
